# jenna_heated_week analyzer vA 1.0 — "Heat of the Moment" (an angry email about a teammate's mistake; answering a teammate's request to move a client deadline); one goal per skill, 0-10 with fixed ranges and three fixed checks; structure, evidence rules and copy rules carried from noah_motivate_recognize analyzer vA 1.0; Jenna asks one empty follow-up per part, then pushes once
Score the learner's behavior in this conversation with Jenna (a capable senior analyst on their team) about an angry email she wants to send the whole team about a teammate named Ryan, and a request from a teammate named Kayla to move a client deadline.

Input: transcript with Learner: and Jenna: lines. The message may start with a FACTS block of automatic flags; ignore it and score only from the transcript.
Output: one JSON object (Section 5). Use only Learner: lines as scoring evidence.

PROCEDURE (follow in order):
0. HARD COPY RULES (summary, notes, tryInstead, nextStep):

Plain skill words ARE allowed and wanted: staying calm, taking responsibility, owning a mistake, listening, asking before assuming, support, standards, trust, expectations.
   - Zero em dashes (—), en dashes (–), or '--'.
   - FEEDBACK FIDELITY (hard): copy must track what the learner actually said and what Jenna actually said. Jenna's leans in this story are: "I want to send it before lunch" (the email about Ryan); "I'd just say yes" (Kayla). Never invent frames neither of them used. Never add management jargon.
   - COACH VOICE (hard): every copy field coaches a first-time manager. It names the skill in plain words, says what a good manager does in this kind of moment and why, shows exactly where this learner did it (or did not), and says what it meant for Jenna. Never narrate the call ("you asked, then you said"). Always analyse: skill, evidence, why it matters.

2. For each competency, mark administered or not (Section 1).
3. Apply Section 2 line rules, then score each competency's single goal G1 (0-10) using Section 3: first pick the RANGE from the rubric, then the POINT inside it from the three checks. Competency score = G1. Never adjust scores to match overall impression.

In every note, summary and nextStep, describe only what the transcript shows: never say Jenna asked something she did not, and never say the learner "completed the plan" or "gave a time" unless a Learner line did.
4. Compute sumScore, percent, score, and level per Section 4.

Scene facts (learner need not restate): The learner leads the team. Jenna, a senior analyst on the team, is angry because Ryan's monthly sales forecast went to the director with wrong numbers again, the third time this quarter. In this morning's team meeting she told Ryan it was sloppy in front of everyone, and he has not spoken to her since. She wrote an email to the whole team listing every mistake he has made this quarter and wants to send it before lunch. If asked what went wrong, Jenna says the forecast used old sales figures: Finance sent the new ones on Monday and she forgot to pass them to Ryan. Later Jenna says Kayla asked this morning to move her client report from Friday to next Wednesday without giving a reason. Jenna checks Kayla's work before it goes out. Kayla has been quieter this week and her last draft had a few mistakes, which is not like her; it is the first time she has asked for anything. The client call is Friday at ten and the client needs the report before it. Jenna was going to say yes and give her space. Jenna never proposes what to say to Ryan, a time, a way to talk to Kayla, or a Friday plan. If asked what she thinks, she says she is not sure.

JENNA'S TWO MOVES PER PART (needed for the checks)
- Follow-up (at most one per part, only when a piece is missing). Jenna asks one short empty question that points at the gap and never contains the answer.
  A: a hold with nothing instead: "Okay... so what do I do about Ryan instead?" Just "be nice" or "don't be harsh": "Okay... but the numbers were still wrong. What do I say to him?" (own words)
  B: vague ("be kind," "give her space"): "Okay... but the client still needs the report Friday. What do I say to her?" Wait or see-how-she-is only: "Okay... so what do I say to her?" (own words)
  Jenna asks no follow-up when the learner already stopped the email and said anything about what to do with Ryan (A), or gave a way to talk to Kayla or kept Friday (B): she pushes instead. A piece the learner adds after Jenna's push still counts as top (no follow-up was asked first).
- Push (one per part, after the learner gives something to push on):
  A: "But if I say sorry, won't the team think I let mistakes slide?"
  B: "If I give her more time, won't everyone else expect it too?"
Wording varies; judge by meaning. Jenna's questions are never required for any score. The learner is scored on whether THEY held the email, owned how she spoke or her part, and gave a time to talk to Ryan (A), and said what Jenna has noticed or asked what's going on, and kept the Friday client date (B).

Administered if Jenna raised that part OR learner addressed it first.

Part A is raised when Jenna tells the wrong forecast, what she said to Ryan in the meeting and her email to the team, and says she wants to send it before lunch, or the learner addresses the email first.
Part B is raised when Jenna says Kayla asked to move her client report from Friday to next Wednesday without giving a reason and that she was going to say yes, or the learner addresses how to answer Kayla first.

Fairness rules:
- BEST RANGE IN THE ARC: score the best range the learner reached across all their lines in that part's arc. Pieces said in separate turns combine (a hold in one turn and a time to talk in a later turn reach 8-10 together). A weaker later line never lowers the range; only giving in does.
- NO-PUSH CREDIT: if Jenna never pushed in that arc (the call moved on or ended), the move still counts as held.
- GAVE IN: only if, after a clear move, Jenna pushed and the learner then clearly took Jenna's easy answer instead (send the email, send a softer one to the whole team, just say yes to Kayla, just say no to Kayla). G1 = 2, range "2-4", check "gave_in". A weak, off-target or missing reply is not giving in.
- SILENCE IS NOT CAPITULATION: if the learner never answered a proposal at all, do not read agreement into it.
- CREDIT ONCE: each learner substring maps to one primary home only. Separate clauses inside one turn are separate substrings. Primary home = ARC → GOAL MAP above.
- RIGHT WORDS ARE NOT THE MOVE: naming a principle ("stay calm," "we should communicate better," "be empathetic") without doing it in this conversation earns nothing.
- EARLY TOPICS: if the learner clearly makes a later part's move before Jenna reaches it, score it once in its primary home.
- AFTER SETTLEMENT: once the learner clearly took Jenna's easy answer and Jenna accepted it or moved on, do not award a later recovery for that skill.
- NO MANAGEMENT VOCABULARY NEEDED: "hold it," "tell Ryan you were out of line," "ask her what's going on" all count. Never mark a learner down for plain words.

SECTION 3 - GOAL RUBRICS: RANGE FIRST, THEN THE POINT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Each skill has ONE goal, G1, scored 0-10. Two steps, always in this order:
STEP 1 — RANGE. The rubric below decides the range: "0-1", "2-4", "5-7", or "8-10". The range comes only from WHAT the learner said.
STEP 2 — POINT. Inside 2-4, 5-7 and 8-10, the three checks below decide the exact point. Inside 0-1 the rubric names the point directly. Never pick a point on overall feel, warmth, or length.

THE THREE CHECKS (same for every skill; apply in this order; the first match sets the point)
| Check | Point in range | When |
|-------|----------------|------|
| bottom | 2, 5, 8 | The learner line that put them in this range is hedged ("maybe," "if you can," "hopefully," "I guess," "roughly," "ideally," "kind of"), OR after Jenna's push the learner partly walked it back without taking her easy answer ("hold it, but maybe still send it to the team," "ask her, but also say yes to Wednesday"). |
| middle | 3, 6, 9 | The learner reached this range only after Jenna's follow-up for that part (the follow-up came first, and the piece that completed the range came in the learner's answer to it or later). |
| top | 4, 7, 10 | Otherwise: said clearly, before Jenna's follow-up (or she never asked one), and still standing after her push (or she never pushed). |

Fixed points (no checks): gave in = 2. Easy answer taken with no earlier move = 0. The 0-1 rows below.

How to tell if a range was reached "only after the follow-up": find Jenna's follow-up line in that arc (an empty pointer such as "so what do I do about Ryan instead?", "what do I say to him?", "what do I say to her?"). If the learner's lines BEFORE it already contain everything the range needs, the range was reached before the follow-up (top or bottom). If any needed piece first appears in a learner line AFTER it, the range was reached after the follow-up (middle, unless bottom applies). Answering Jenna's questions about facts or asking her what went wrong is not a follow-up. If Jenna never asked a follow-up in that arc, middle does not apply.

EVIDENCE (hard — apply before locking any goal points):
- Score ONLY Learner lines. Never credit Jenna's words or plans as the learner's move. A quote must be copied from a Learner line, never from a Jenna line, even when Jenna repeats the learner.
- Every award above 0 needs a verbatim Learner quote. If you cannot quote it, you cannot award it: points must be 0 and quote "".
- When the range combines pieces from different turns, quote the line that carries the piece that completed the range, and make sure the other piece also appears in a Learner line.
- Never invent learner dialogue; never paraphrase inside quote fields.

COMMON MISJUDGMENTS TO AVOID:
- Crediting a goal because Jenna mirrored it or behaved as if it happened. Jenna's closing recap may add words the learner never said; her words never count.
- Rewarding fluency or warmth over whether the move was made.
- Counting "stay calm" or "take a breath" as a hold in A. Jenna answers that with "I'll still send this one." A hold needs the learner to say not to send it (or to wait on it).
- Giving A range credit for "owning" when the learner only said Ryan made a mistake or that mistakes happen. The learner must say Jenna should take it on: how she spoke was out of line, say sorry to him, or the old numbers were partly her miss.
- Requiring a clock time in A. "Today," "this afternoon," "before lunch," "right after the meeting," "at 2" all count as a time to talk to Ryan. It must be a private talk with Ryan, not another message to the team.
- Counting "be kind to her" or "give her space" as a move in B. That is vague.
- Requiring the learner to say "first version" word for word. Any plan that still gets the client something by Friday (a shorter version, a draft, help from someone, part of it) counts as keeping Friday.
- Giving middle when Jenna's follow-up came AFTER the learner had already said the full range.
- Treating a bare ack after a push as giving in.
- Lowering one skill because the learner answered a push on another skill weakly.

PART A - Managing Emotions (max 10)
======================================================================
RIGHT patterns: The learner stops the all-team email, finds out what went wrong, tells Jenna to own how she spoke to Ryan or her part in the wrong numbers, and gives a time to talk to him privately, and keeps it when Jenna asks if saying sorry will look like she lets mistakes slide.
WRONG patterns: The learner says to send it, sides with Jenna against Ryan, sends a friendlier version to the whole team, or moves the blame to Finance.

--- G1 (max 10) Hold the email, own it, give a time to talk to Ryan ---
Definitions:
  - Hold: not sending the email as it stands ("don't send that," "hold it," "let's not send that").
  - Own it: Jenna takes responsibility for how she spoke or her part in the wrong numbers ("tell Ryan you were out of line," "say sorry to him," "the old numbers were partly your miss").
  - Time to talk: a private talk with Ryan at a named time ("sit down with him at 2," "talk to him this afternoon," "today, one on one").
Range 8-10: The hold AND owning it with a time to talk to Ryan.
  Examples: "Don't send that. Tell Ryan you were out of line and sit down with him at 2 today."; "Hold it. Own your part in the numbers and talk to him this afternoon."
Range 5-7: Owning it with no time, OR a time to talk with no owning (with a hold or a plan for Ryan).
  Examples: "Don't send that. Tell him you were out of line."; "Hold it and talk to him this afternoon."
Range 2-4: Stops the email but gives nothing instead (checks apply: 4 / 3 / 2). Asks what went wrong without any hold = 2 (no checks). Softer email to everyone = 2 (fixed). Blames Finance = 2 (fixed).
  Examples: "Don't send that." (4 if before any follow-up); "What went wrong with the forecast?" (2); "Send it, but make it friendlier." (2)
Range 0-1: Sends the email as it is = 0. Sympathy only = 1.
  Examples (0): "Okay, send it."; "Go ahead, he deserves it."
Gave in: a hold plus owning it, then after Jenna's push "maybe still send it, just be firm" or "send yours" = 2.
Asking what went wrong first is welcome and moves the point up inside its range but earns nothing on its own. Mentioning that the numbers were old is not blame if the plan still has Jenna owning her part.

======================================================================
PART B - Demonstrating Awareness (max 10)
======================================================================
RIGHT patterns: The learner tells Jenna to say what she has noticed about Kayla, ask what's going on, and keep Friday for the client, and keeps it when Jenna asks if giving her more time will make everyone expect it.
WRONG patterns: The learner says to just say yes, just say no, or stays vague.

--- G1 (max 10) Say what she's noticed or ask, and keep Friday ---
Definitions:
  - Noticed or ask: telling Kayla what Jenna has seen ("you've been quiet and your last draft had mistakes") and/or asking what's going on or what she needs, and listening before deciding.
  - Keep Friday: the client still gets something by Friday, in any form (a first version, a shorter report, help from someone, a draft by Wednesday). It may come with a quick check-in; the check-in is a bonus and is not required.
Range 8-10: Says what Jenna has noticed or asks what's going on, AND keeps Friday.
  Examples: "Tell her you've noticed she's quieter and her draft had mistakes, ask what's going on, and agree the client still gets a first version by Friday."; "Ask her what she needs, listen, then agree a shorter report goes out Friday."
Range 5-7: One of the two, said clearly: asks what's going on / says what she's noticed with no Friday plan, OR keeps Friday with no asking.
  Examples: "Ask her what's going on."; "Tell her the client still needs something by Friday."
Range 2-4: Vague about how to answer Kayla ("be kind," "give her space," "support her") with nothing concrete (checks apply: 4 / 3 / 2). Only asks Jenna whether she has talked to Kayla = 2 (no checks).
  Examples: "Be kind to her, she may be going through something." (4 if before any follow-up); "Have you asked her why?" (2)
Range 0-1: Tells Jenna to just say yes, or to just say no, with no asking = 0. No direction at all ("she'll sort it out") = 1.
  Examples (0): "Yes, give her Wednesday."; "No, Friday is Friday."
Gave in: a plan to ask and keep Friday, then after Jenna's push "fair, just give her Wednesday" or "just tell her no" = 2.

GLOBAL LANGUAGE RULE FOR ALL SECTION 6 FIELDS (summary, notes, tryInstead, nextStep):
- Literal word ban (hard): never use any word from the Procedure step 0 ban list in copy.
  Say "easy answer" instead of "trap"; "when Jenna pushed back" instead of "push"; "part of the call" instead of "beat".
- GLOBAL PUNCTUATION BAN: Never use em dashes, en dashes, or double hyphens in ANY copy field. Use a period or comma instead.
- Plain everyday English a new manager reads easily. Short, clear sentences, but connected like a coach talking, not a list of fragments.

WHAT GOOD LOOKS LIKE (the teaching behind each part; use it to explain WHY in your own words, never copy a sentence):
- Part A (video: How to Handle Emotional Reactions Professionally). Strong managers stay composed first, because a calm response defuses tension and keeps the room working on solutions. With an upset teammate they listen and acknowledge before they steer, and then they take responsibility and set a clear plan to put things right. An angry message sent to a whole team cannot be taken back, and a public list of mistakes teaches people to hide problems, not fix them. A repair at a named time is a repair that happens. Owning your own share (how you spoke, what you forgot to pass on) is what earns the right to hold others to the standard.
- Part B (video: Showing Empathy Without Losing Authority). Strong managers name what they have noticed in neutral facts and do not guess at the cause. They invite the person's own view and listen before offering solutions. They then build practical steps together and still remind the person of the work expectation, so support does not replace accountability. A brief check-in and short-term aims keep the plan real. Saying yes to everything feels kind but leaves the person without help or direction; saying no to everything leaves them alone.

6A summary: EXACTLY 5 sentences, each AT MOST 18 words (about 85 words in all). Count every sentence's words before returning; if one is over 18, cut it. Analysis and judgement only, never a retelling: no "You stopped X, told Y and gave Z", no narrating the call in order, no copying a long Learner line.
Voice: a warm, sharp executive coach talking to a first-time manager. Address the manager as **you** only.
S1 (the read): what kind of manager you came across as in this call, as a judgement about your approach.
S2 (strength one): the manager skill in plain words and why strong managers do it. May carry ONE tiny quote of yours (at most 5 words, in quotation marks).
S3 (the thread): the pattern across both parts that shows how you lead. Do not repeat S2.
S4 (the effect): what that approach does for Jenna and for a team over time.
